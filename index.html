MODULE 1

What is Phishing?

Phishing is a type of cyberattack where attackers pretend to be trustworthy people or organizations to trick users into revealing sensitive information or performing unsafe actions.

Common targets: Passwords, banking information, OTPs, personal information, and account credentials.
MODULE 2

How to Identify Suspicious Emails

Phishing emails often try to make you act quickly without thinking. Look for these warning signs:

  • Unexpected messages from unknown senders
  • Urgent or threatening language
  • Suspicious links or unexpected attachments
  • Requests for passwords, OTPs, or financial information
  • Unusual sender addresses or spelling mistakes
  • Offers that seem too good to be true
Example:

"Your account will be permanently suspended in 30 minutes. Click here immediately to verify your account."

Security Tip: Don't click immediately. Verify the message through the organization's official website or another trusted channel.

MODULE 3

Fake Login Pages

Attackers may create fake login pages that look similar to legitimate services. Their goal is to trick users into entering usernames and passwords.

Check the website address

  • Check the domain name carefully.
  • Look for misspellings or unexpected domains.
  • Be cautious of shortened or suspicious URLs.
  • Don't assume HTTPS automatically means a website is legitimate.
Example:

A fake website might use a domain that looks similar to a legitimate service but contains additional words or misspellings.
MODULE 4

Fraudulent Websites

Fraudulent websites are designed to look legitimate and may attempt to steal information, money, or account credentials.

  • Check the exact domain name.
  • Be cautious of unexpected redirects.
  • Don't enter sensitive information on suspicious websites.
  • Verify contact information independently.
  • Be careful with unrealistic discounts and offers.
MODULE 5

Social Engineering Techniques

Social engineering manipulates people into taking actions that benefit an attacker.

Urgency

Creating pressure so the victim acts without verifying the request.

Authority

Pretending to be a manager, bank, government agency, or other trusted authority.

Fear

Threatening account suspension, financial loss, or other negative consequences.

Curiosity

Using interesting or unexpected content to encourage users to click malicious links.

Baiting

Offering something attractive to encourage a user to perform an unsafe action.

Impersonation

Pretending to be someone the victim knows or trusts.

MODULE 6

How to Stay Safe

  • Think before clicking links or opening attachments.
  • Verify unexpected requests through trusted channels.
  • Check website addresses carefully.
  • Use multi-factor authentication (MFA).
  • Use strong and unique passwords.
  • Consider using a reputable password manager.
  • Keep your operating system and applications updated.
  • Report suspected phishing attempts.
Remember: Legitimate organizations generally will not ask you to reveal passwords or authentication codes through unexpected messages.
MODULE 7

Real-Life Phishing Case Studies

Case Study 1: Google and Facebook Business Email Scam

Between 2013 and 2015, an attacker used fraudulent invoices and impersonation to trick Google and Facebook employees into sending payments to bank accounts controlled by the attacker.

Warning Signs

  • Impersonation of a trusted business partner
  • Fraudulent invoices and payment requests
  • Social engineering designed to appear legitimate
Lesson: Verify unusual payment requests independently, even when the request appears to come from a trusted organization.

Case Study 2: Twitter Social Engineering Attack

In 2020, attackers used social engineering to target Twitter employees and gain access to internal tools. The attackers then used compromised accounts to promote a cryptocurrency scam.

Warning Signs

  • Targeting employees through social engineering
  • Abuse of privileged internal access
  • Use of compromised accounts for fraudulent activity
Lesson: Organizations should protect privileged access and train employees to recognize social engineering attempts.

Case Study 3: Business Email Compromise

Business Email Compromise (BEC) attacks commonly involve attackers impersonating executives, suppliers, or employees and requesting money transfers or sensitive information.

Warning Signs

  • Unexpected requests for money transfers
  • Urgent requests that bypass normal procedures
  • Changes to payment details
  • Requests for confidential information
Lesson: Follow established verification procedures for financial and sensitive requests instead of relying only on email.
